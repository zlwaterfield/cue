Why I built it

I wanted one place to put the day. Not a project manager or a second brain, just a page for today that I could open and type into without deciding where anything goes first.

Every tool I tried wanted a project, a tag or a priority before it would take a note. So I built Cue, for myself: days are the only structure, the keyboard does everything, and it’s ready before I’ve finished thinking of what to write.

What makes it different

The day is the only folder

Things belong to dates, not projects. No tags, priorities or dashboards. Whatever didn’t get done moves to tomorrow with ⌘D.

Keyboard first

Tab nests, ⌘↩ completes, and ⌘K does everything else, including dates like “move to fri”. The mouse still works.

Fast, and measured

Ready to type 240 ms after launch, even with five years of notes. A keystroke renders in 1.5 ms. Every number has a budget and a test.

On your Mac and your iPhone

Saved as you type, with no telemetry, and the Mac exports to Markdown or JSON any time. Neither app needs an account; turn on sync and the same notes are on both. Free, both ways.

Cue stays small on purpose: no reminders, recurring tasks, collaboration or AI in the app.

Your assistant can use it too

There’s no AI inside Cue, and there won’t be. There’s a door instead: Cue runs an MCP server, so Claude, ChatGPT or whatever you already talk to can read your days, add to-dos, check them off and move them. What it does lands on your Mac and your iPhone about a second later, exactly as if you’d typed it.

Five tools, none of which can delete anything. It needs sync on, you connect it once at https://www.usecue.today/mcp, and you can disconnect it whenever you like.

Where it’s at

It’s in beta, and it’s free. Cue is my daily driver on my Mac and my iPhone, and it runs without any issues. The Mac app is a free download that works offline with no account, and the iPhone app is in beta on TestFlight.

Want the same notes on both? Sign in with Apple or your email and they sync, free as well. That’s the only part with a server in it — a Postgres database at Supabase, and the MCP endpoint, and nothing else. No tracking either way, and the code is open if you’d rather build it yourself.

Questions

Is Cue really free?

Yes. There’s no paid plan, no trial, no credit card and no upsell. Sync is free too. It’s a personal project, not a business, and I don’t plan to charge for it.

Is it open source?

All of it, under the MIT license: the Mac app, the iPhone app, the sync server and this website. Read it, build it, or run your own copy — it’s on GitHub.

Does it work offline?

Completely. Both apps keep your notes on the device and need no network at all. With sync off, Cue never talks to a server.

Do I need an account?

Only if you want the same notes on more than one device. Each app is complete on its own: open it and type.

Can my assistant use Cue?

Yes, over MCP. Claude, ChatGPT or another assistant can read your days, add to-dos, check them off and move them, and what it does syncs to your devices like any other edit. It can’t delete anything. There’s no AI inside the app itself.

Where are my notes kept?

On the Mac, in one SQLite file at ~/Library/Application Support/Cue/cue.sqlite, with a daily snapshot kept for 14 days. On the iPhone, in the app’s own database. Export everything from the Mac, as Markdown or JSON, whenever you like.

Does Cue track me?

No analytics, no telemetry, no crash reporting, no ads, and no cookies on this website. Your notes are never training data for an AI model, and nothing is sold or shared. The privacy policy is short, because there isn’t much to say.

What servers are involved?

With sync off, none at all. With it on, two: a Postgres database at Supabase that holds your notes, where row-level security means an account can only ever read its own rows, and the MCP endpoint your assistant talks to. There is nothing else, and no third service watching.

How fast is it?

Ready to type 240 ms after launch with five years of notes, 1.5 ms from a keystroke to the frame, and 0.5 ms to search 36,543 items. Every number has a budget that a test fails if it misses, measured on the real packaged app.

What doesn’t it do?

No projects, tags, priorities, folders, reminders, recurring tasks, attachments, collaboration or dashboards. If you want a project manager or a second brain, Cue is the wrong tool, and would rather say so than grow into one.
